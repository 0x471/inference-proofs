    Glossary

    The ladder's words and the field's.

    GlossaryABCEFGHIKLMNOPTQRSVWZ

    A

    Abstraction of the protocol

    What a machine-checked proof covers, in three levels: an abstraction of the protocol, the method without this model's computation (or the model without the method); the protocol, the method with the model's computation; the code this row runs, the verifier program the row actually runs. Only the verifier the row runs counts as the code. A proof about a formal model of the method, rather than about a program, verifies the protocol (or an abstraction of it), never the code.

    Adapter

    The wrapper through which the harness runs a system's commands, in a container built at the system's pinned commit. It passes no setting the system's documentation does not name (what we do).

    AIR

    An algebraic intermediate representation: a computation as a table of values (the trace) with polynomial constraints between rows. STARK provers such as Winterfell (new tab) take a computation in this form.

    Architecture

    A model's layout and sizes: its layers, widths and heads. It is public for every Level 1 model, so no Level 1 petal grades hiding it.

    Attestation

    A report, signed by hardware or an organisation, that a condition holds. In policy writing, an attestation is a formal declaration that a standard is met.

    B

    Bar

    The most faithful damaged copy that every honest copy scores above; a system's proved replies must score above it to pass the Fidelity check. Its rule is on the checks page.

    Bound

    Said of a link in the computation that the verifier's acceptance constrains, such as the weights to their commitment. A claim card records what is bound as the publisher states it, or reads “not stated”. As a noun, a bound is a stated limit, such as on the probability that a false proof is accepted.

    C

    Check

    One of the six tests of Level 1: Run, Model, Prompt, Reply, Verify without weights and Fidelity. Each ends passed, failed or not decided, and a check not decided names its cause: the system cannot do what the test needs, not run yet, the test ran and its result does not count yet, or a changed input accepted in our adapter and not yet repeated with the system's own command.

    Claim card

    The record of what one system's accepted proofs establish in one configuration, as its publisher states it. Each field is copied from the publisher's documents at the pinned commit and linked, or reads “not stated” (RFC-0002 (new tab)).

    Commit and proving

    The part of a whole session's GPU time that is not generation: the commit call, which commits to the weights, and the proving of every reply. Under Cost vs serving it is printed beside generation, each over the same serving time.

    Commitment

    A value computed from data, such as a model's weights, that fixes them for later proofs without publishing them. The verifier takes it from the harness's record, never from files sent with a proof. In policy writing, a commitment is a pledge.

    Completeness

    The property that a verifier accepts every proof an honest prover makes of a true statement, often except with a small stated probability. It protects the provider, and it is the one of the three properties a test can show: on a system's page it reads “tested by us on honest runs” with the count of honest replies whose proofs were accepted (Run check). No petal grades it. In safeguards writing, a complete declaration leaves nothing out.

    Confidentiality

    Whether checking a proof reveals the model's weights, graded on the zero knowledge the publisher states and proves (petal). It protects the provider. At Level 1 the architecture is visible, since the models are open-weights.

    Computed by our code from recorded numbers

    How a derived figure was obtained: code calculates it from the times and counts our harness recorded, and no one types it. Its title names the records it is computed from (RFC-0004 (new tab)).

    Conjectured

    Said of a soundness bound that rests on an unproved conjecture. On the Integrity petal, a conjectured bound reaches Partly met at most.

    Control

    An input changed on purpose (a negative control), from which a working system gives no accepted proof. A trial counts only if its honest twin, the trial unchanged, is accepted. The fidelity run's controls are runs beside the original, honest or damaged.

    Cost vs serving

    How many times the GPU time of serving the same reply without a proof, at full load on the same GPU type, a proved reply takes. Both sides are GPU-seconds per reply, the proved side over whole sessions (generation, commit and proving), so the GPU price is the same on both sides; the rule says how each side is timed.

    E

    Evidence: our runs only, not rerun by anyone else

    We measured it on our own sessions, and no one else has rerun them yet. Every Level 1 figure has this evidence; a figure rerun by an unaffiliated team would say so (RFC-0007 (new tab)).

    Exact match

    An acceptance policy under which each checked token must be the committed model's top token. A policy with a tolerance also accepts other tokens.

    F

    Failed

    For a petal: one of our checks, or our build of the publisher's proof, contradicted it. For a check, see failed (check).

    Failed (check)

    A check whose test showed the opposite of what it needs, repeated with the system's own command where a changed input was accepted in our adapter.

    Fiat-Shamir

    A transform that makes an interactive proof non-interactive by hashing the statement and transcript into each challenge.

    Fidelity

    How closely the copy a system proves answers like the model its publisher released. One name covers three things, told apart by a word: the Fidelity check and the Fidelity petal count matched leading tokens, and the Fidelity figure counts top-token agreement. Beside a figure, the site prints the quantization the system proves.

    Fidelity check

    One of the six checks: the proved replies match the original's opening tokens better than the bar does, by a paired test at p < 0.05.

    Fidelity figure

    The share of reply positions where the system, fed the prompt and the original's reply, picks the original's next token (top-token agreement). It sets no state and is labelled “computed by the system's own code on the reference reply, not by the proved computation”.

    Field

    A finite set of numbers, such as the integers modulo a prime, with exact addition, multiplication and division. Proof systems compute in one because their random checks need a product of two nonzero numbers never to be 0.

    Formally verified

    How far a computer-checked (machine-checked) proof of soundness reaches (petal): part of the protocol, an abstraction of it, the protocol with the model, or the verifier code the row runs. Our recorded build of the named proof decides the step. It protects the customer.

    FRI

    A protocol that shows committed values lie close to a Reed-Solomon codeword, the evaluations of a low-degree polynomial. Its authors built it (new tab) for proof systems that “require only public randomness for their setup”.

    G

    Generation

    The phase of a session in which the system's own model computes the replies, where the system does this apart from proving them. Its GPU time counts in the proved side of Cost vs serving, and the figure prints it as a part of its own.

    Glyph

    A five-petal figure of how close a system is to a perfect prover. Its petals, every one Met for such a prover, are Integrity, Confidentiality, Fidelity, Formally verified and Trust assumptions. Nothing adds them up, and the glyph never changes a system's position.

    GPU-second

    One GPU busy for one second. GPU-seconds add over every GPU a job holds, so two GPUs for one second are two GPU-seconds.

    Greedy decoding

    Choosing the highest-scoring token at every step. With exact arithmetic and a fixed rule for ties, the same model and prompt always give the same reply.

    H

    Halfway point

    The average of two copies' mean scores; a row is shown above it by a paired sign-flip test at p < 0.05. The Fidelity petal's steps are halfway points between the original run again, the 8-bit copy and the 4-bit copy.

    Honest and damaged copies

    The fidelity run's yardsticks, fixed in the level file before any run: honest copies are the original run again and its 8-bit and 4-bit copies, the usual ways to run the model; damaged copies (3-bit and 2-bit copies, a quarter of the model's blocks cut, a smaller model) are made worse on purpose, and the highest-scoring of them that every honest copy scores above is the bar.

    Harness

    The benchmark's software (the Python package iproofs), which runs each adapter in a container and times it from outside.

    I

    Inference

    Running a trained model forward on an input to produce an output. A language model writes its reply one token at a time.

    Integrity

    Whether a provider can get a reply accepted that the model did not compute, graded on the soundness bound the publisher states for what the row runs (petal). It protects the customer. It grades soundness only: completeness is what the Run check tests.

    K

    KL divergence

    A measure of how far one probability distribution lies from another (Kullback-Leibler divergence). KL to the original weights is printed and gates nothing.

    L

    Ladder

    The home page: one table per open-weights model, largest first, with one row per system run on it. Systems are ranked inside a model, never across models.

    Level

    A frozen definition of the benchmark in one file: the models, request, checks, order rule and constants. The glyph's rules are not part of it, and changing them needs no new level (new tab).

    Lookup argument

    A proof that every value in a long list appears in a fixed public table. Provers use it for the steps of a model that need more than additions and multiplications.

    M

    Machine-checked

    Said of a proof checked by a proof assistant's kernel, such as Lean's. What it takes on trust, such as the kernel and its axioms, is its trusted base. The Formally verified petal grades how far such a proof of soundness reaches.

    Met, Mostly met, Partly met

    A petal's top step, the step below it, and the lowest step above Not met. Each petal's rule on the checks page says what each needs. The steps are drawn as inks; no printed word names an ink.

    Matched leading tokens

    How many tokens at the start of a reply equal the original's, up to the first difference; the score of the Fidelity check.

    Mixture of experts

    A model that runs only part of its weights for each token. Llama 4 Maverick runs about 17 billion of its 400 billion parameters.

    Model check

    A proof made with one weight changed is not accepted against the original weights' commitment (rule).

    Multilinear extension

    The one polynomial, linear in each variable, that takes a table's values on the corners of the boolean hypercube.

    Multiple

    The ranked figure: steady GPU-seconds per further proved reply over the serving baseline's GPU-seconds per reply at full load. The ladder prints it as Cost vs serving, with generation counted. A row with no steady state has none and prints its whole sessions' cost over serving.

    N

    Next step

    What would move a petal up one step, computed from its rule, and who acts: the publisher or us. A petal not decided prints what decides it.

    Not decided

    A petal we cannot grade yet; its line says what it waits on, and it is never a failure.

    Not decided (check)

    A check that has not run, ran without a result that counts yet, or that the system cannot run. Its line says what would decide it and who acts.

    Not met

    A petal's step when the publisher's own facts fall below Partly met.

    Not stated

    A petal's step when the publisher's documents read at the pinned commit say nothing on it. A guarantee nobody states is graded as none.

    Number format

    Whether the proved copy computes in integers, fixed point (integers with a fixed scale) or floating point, and with how many bits per weight.

    O

    Operator

    The party that serves a model and makes its proofs, such as a lab or an API provider. RFC-0018 (new tab) also uses the word for the team that runs this benchmark.

    P

    Passed

    Said of a system on a model when all six of our checks passed there. Not passed yet: no check failed, but at least one is not decided. Not passed: a check failed. Not ranked: passed, without a position, for the reason printed beside it.

    Passed (check)

    A check whose test came out as its rule requires: for a check that changes an input, the change we made was refused.

    Pinned commit

    The full commit hash at which a system is built, run and quoted. It is unrelated to a commitment, which fixes a model's weights.

    Polynomial commitment

    A commitment to a polynomial that the prover opens at any point the verifier picks, with a short proof. It lets a verifier check claims about a model's weights without holding them.

    Position

    A ranked system's place in its model's table: 1 plus the number of systems placed above it. A system has one only if it passed and its publisher states a numeric security level at the pinned commit, for at least one verifier it ships.

    Prompt check

    With one prompt token changed, the verifier refuses the proof (rule).

    Proof of inference

    Files that let a verifier check that a reply is what a committed model produces for a prompt. In policy writing, a proof is evidence strong enough to support a conclusion.

    Proved

    Said of a statement that a sound verifier's acceptance establishes, under the claim card's stated assumptions.

    Prover

    The program that makes a proof, and by extension whoever runs it. It holds the witness.

    Publisher

    The organisation that releases a proof system and its documents, or a model and its weights. The model as its publisher released it is the original.

    T

    The publisher's words

    Sentences copied word for word from the publisher's documents at the pinned commit, each linked to its line. They are printed as the publisher's and never set a state alone.

    Token

    The unit of text a model reads and writes, a word or part of one, stored as an integer.

    Tokens per second

    The request's reply tokens over the GPU-seconds one reply took, on one GPU, printed in tokens/s per GPU with the GPU named. A row has two, on the same GPU type: proved, over whole sessions, and served, by the serving baseline at full load. Served over proved is the row's Cost vs serving. They depend on the GPU, so neither ranks a row (RFC-0018 (new tab) CO-8).

    Trust assumptions

    Whether checking a proof relies on anyone's honesty besides the mathematics, such as a setup secret or a hardware maker's key (petal). It protects the customer.

    Trusted execution environment

    A hardware enclave (TEE) in a processor that isolates code and memory from the machine's owner. It signs a report of what it loaded, an attestation that rests on the chip maker's keys and an untampered chip.

    Trusted setup

    A one-time step that makes public parameters from a secret whose holder could forge proofs, as in KZG commitments. The Trust assumptions petal is Met when the publisher states that soundness rests on cryptographic assumptions alone and no setup produced a secret.

    Q

    Q8_0 and Q4_K_M

    The 8-bit copy (Q8_0) and the 4-bit copy (Q4_K_M): the original model with its weights rounded to 8 bits, and mostly to 4 bits, by llama.cpp at the level's pinned commit. We run them without a proof, as yardsticks, and the ladder prints their Fidelity figure under each system's.

    Quantization

    Storing a model's numbers in fewer bits, which is cheaper to prove and can change replies. Each system on the ladder proves a quantized copy of the original; the Fidelity figure shows how far its replies move.

    R

    Random oracle

    An ideal hash function that gives every new input a fresh, uniformly random output. Fiat-Shamir proofs are usually proved sound with the hash taken as one.

    Read as

    What a petal rests on, printed beside its step: stated, the publisher's own sentence; read as, our reading of sentences none of which says it alone; tested by us, our own runs. A petal read as prints the quotes it rests on and is graded Mostly met at most, for every system alike. The name of a construction, such as STARK, is never read as a claim.

    Recomputation

    Checking a claimed output by running the computation again and comparing. It needs the weights, hardware to run them, and arithmetic that two honest runs agree on or a tolerance between them.

    Relying party

    Whoever acts on a verifier's verdict, such as a customer, an auditor, a regulator or an inspectorate. It runs the verifier or trusts someone who does, and judges what a verdict is worth.

    Reply check

    Every proof covers all of the request's reply tokens, and with one changed (first, last or one inside) the verifier refuses it (rule).

    Request

    One prompt and the reply checked for it: at Level 1, 512 prompt tokens and a 512-token greedy reply.

    Residual

    What a dishonest prover can still do while its proofs are accepted. FlashProve's claim card quotes its publisher's known limits, and SASH's reads “not stated”.

    Run check

    The publisher's prover, as published at the pin, ran three sessions from a cold start, and every reply got an accepted proof (rule).

    S

    Sampled

    Said of a guarantee that comes from checking a random part of the work, such as some replies. It is statistical, not cryptographic, and certifies no reply left unchecked.

    Security level

    A measure, in bits, of how hard it is to get a false proof accepted. It is not a fixed chance per attempt: the soundness error usually grows with the forger's work. FlashProve states q/2124 + 2-101 after q hash queries, so each further query adds to a forger's chance. A stated level's basis is proved, conjectured or not said, and Level 1 prints the level as stated.

    Serving baseline

    The GPU-seconds per reply that serving the request takes with no proof, on the model publisher's own release. It is measured at full load.

    Session

    One run of a system from a cold start on one slot's prompts: it loads the model, commits to the weights, then proves every reply (120 replies on Llama 4 Maverick, 5 on Llama 3.1 8B). Three complete sessions count, one per slot.

    Slot

    One of three fixed sets of a model's prompts. Each slot counts its first complete session, and a failed session is rerun in the same slot.

    SNARK and STARK

    Two names for proof systems: SNARKs (succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge) and STARKs. A STARK is typically built from hash functions, with no trusted setup. Neither name means that the proof hides anything (zero knowledge).

    Soundness

    The property that a verifier refuses proofs of false statements, except with a probability the security level bounds. It protects the customer, and Integrity grades the bound its publisher states. It rests on a security proof under stated assumptions. A test can refute it but never establish it.

    Soundness error

    The probability, as the publisher states it, that a verifier accepts a proof of a false statement: the bound behind soundness. It is stated for a given verifier, per proof or per part of one, often as a function of the forger's hash queries. The Integrity petal grades it: its reason names every condition that keeps it from covering what the row runs, then the bound, such as a verifier other than the one the bound is stated for.

    Statement

    The proposition that an accepted proof establishes. For an inference prover: the model fixed by this commitment, run on this prompt under this decoding rule, produces this reply.

    Steady state

    The part of a session after its first group of proofs, when proofs come out at an even pace. A release group is a burst of proofs released together. A row is steady when its three sessions are.

    Sumcheck

    A protocol that convinces a verifier of a polynomial's sum over exponentially many points with one random evaluation. The verifier's work is one round per variable.

    V

    Verification

    Checking a claim against evidence that holds even if the claimant has a reason to cheat. The word also names judging compliance with a law or a treaty, and proving software correct against a specification. On the ladder it means running a verifier, whose verdict alone never settles compliance.

    Verifier

    The program that checks a proof against the commitment and the prompt, and accepts or refuses. It is given the reply or reads it from the proof. In policy writing, the verifier is the inspectorate or the auditor, here called the relying party.

    Verify without weights check

    The verifier reached every verdict with no copy of the model and no GPU (rule).

    W

    Weights

    The learned numbers a model is made of, about 8 billion in Llama 3.1 8B. For a closed model they are its owner's trade secret.

    Whole session

    A figure that counts a session's GPU time from the start of its commit call to its last proof (the commit, generation and proving; loading the model before the commit is not counted), divided by its replies. Cost vs serving, the session GPU time and the proved tokens per second are figures of whole sessions.

    Witness

    Everything the prover uses to build a proof and the verifier does not see. In a proof of inference: the weights and every layer's activations.

    Z

    Zero knowledge

    The property that a proof shows nothing beyond the truth of its statement: all a verifier sees could be simulated without the weights. It comes in forms. Honest-verifier zero knowledge holds only against a verifier that follows the protocol. After Fiat-Shamir it can hold against any verifier, often within a stated number of hash queries and in the random oracle model. It is perfect, statistical or computational as the simulated view is identical, close up to a stated distance, or only hard to tell apart. What the statement makes public stays visible: the model's sizes, the prompt, the reply. It protects the provider. The Confidentiality petal prints the form the publisher states, asks it of the programs that run, not only of the protocol, and of the row's batches.
