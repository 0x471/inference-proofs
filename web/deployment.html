    Deployments and treaties

    Proving costs a large multiple of serving, and checking needs no weights or trusted chip. A treaty could prove replies sampled from a trusted record.

    TEE.fail (new tab)'s authors forged attestations of Intel's CPU enclaves (SGX, TDX) with a memory-bus interposer costing “under $1000” to build. Intel and AMD leave interposer attacks outside their threat models.

    The same authors write that “the fact that Nvidia's attestation reports are not bound to the identity of a specific CVM means we can “borrow” Nvidia attestations from devices that aren't ours (new tab)” (a CVM is a confidential virtual machine): a GPU attestation does not show which machine ran the workload.

    TEEs and activation checks are cheap, and for many settings they are the right tool; TEEs are a sensible starting point for a first agreement. What they cost is trust: in a hardware vendor, in a third party, or in a tolerance. A proof moves that trust into cryptographic assumptions and into code that both sides can read.

    In an agreement

    Consider a hypothetical bilateral AI agreement between the United States (US) and China: declared data centres in both countries serve only models whose commitments are in a shared register. A jointly agreed inspectorate holds the register and checks an operator in either country, which keeps its weights. This is an illustrative scenario, not a description of an existing deal.

    Given accepted proofs and a trusted traffic record, the inspectorate could sign one finding: each sampled reply is what the registered quantized copy gives for its recorded prompt.

    Run on this site

    This reply is what the registered model gives

    The inspectorate verifies a reply's proof against the registered commitment. That does not show the reply came from live traffic, or that the weights stayed hidden. We run this step on open-weights models, with 512-token prompts and replies.

    Shown in research

    The model that was evaluated is the model that serves

    Proved against one commitment, evaluation replies and served replies are what the same weights give. South and others (new tab) proved evaluations of small models this way in 2024. No proof says whether the evaluation asked the right questions.

    Proposed, parts built

    A sample stands for the traffic

    A certifier at the inspected data centre's boundary, in either country, records a fingerprint of every prompt and reply, and must be trusted. SASH's publisher (new tab) writes: “Nothing yet ties a proof to what the certifier recorded.”

    The inspectorate later draws replies for the operator to prove. SASH's publisher adds that “the draw must be unpredictable to the provider, and the provider must commit to its answers before the draw.” By our arithmetic, when both hold, 300 draws catch an unregistered model serving 1 reply in 100 about 95 % of the time, and 1 in 10,000 about 3 %.

    Any reply may be drawn, so the quantized copy must generate every reply. Where a system's page prints a generation part, that part is paid on every reply, drawn or not. Of the “commit and proving” part, only proving shrinks with the sample.

    Outside a proof's reach

    The data centre did nothing else

    The AI 2040 verification plan (new tab) would keep data centres to inference, and currently recommends aiming for “a network-tap and recomputation based approach”. Amodo Design (new tab) grades the plan's measures: 4 items with an active effort, 6 not started, 7 “Not on track”.

    Outside a proof's reach

    Nothing was left undeclared

    To find undeclared data centres, a RAND working paper (Baker and others, 2025 (new tab)) names chip tracking, satellite images, whistleblowers and national intelligence.
