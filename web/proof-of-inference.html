    What a proof shows

    A proof shows a reply is the committed model's greedy reply to its prompt, not whose model it is or that the reply was served.

    1 of 3

    An operator says which model wrote a reply, and nothing in the reply shows it. Gao, Liang and Guestrin (new tab) tested commercial APIs serving four Llama models in summer 2024 and found that “11 out of 31 endpoints serve different distributions than reference weights released by Meta”.

    A proof of inference proves a narrower claim, one reply at a time. The operator computes a commitment, a value that fixes the model's weights without publishing them. The verifier checks the operator's proof of a reply against the commitment and the prompt, and accepts or refuses it.

    A release, an evaluation report or a register has to tie the commitment to a known model.

    The relying party (a customer, an auditor, a regulator or an inspectorate) judges what an accepted proof is worth. Policy writing calls the inspectorate or auditor in that role the verifier.

    Outside the proof

    • What an operator proves in its own deployment. Our runs show what each system's own prover and verifier did on our request, with the commitment we recorded; they show nothing about the proofs an operator makes of its own traffic, which runs whatever code it chooses outside our tests. A relying party checks each proof itself (what it supplies).
    • When, or whether, the reply was served. A proof made later shows the operator holds a model that gives this reply, “not that it necessarily served it earlier” (South and others, 2024 (new tab)).
    • The model as released. The verifiers on the ladder check a quantized copy (why). No proof says how far its replies drift from the original's: we measure that, and the Fidelity check stops a copy quantized far from the original.
    • A reply with tokens chosen at random. The ladder's proofs cover the greedy reply.
    • What else the machines ran. Anthropic (new tab), which publishes FlashProve, writes that a proof “does not establish that the hardware is not, e.g., training on the side”.
    • That the model is safe, or how it was trained.
    • What surrounds the model. A router, an output filter or a system prompt the verifier is not given can change what a user gets.
    • That the prompt and the reply stay private. The verifier reads them and other values, such as the model's sizes.
    • That a proof will arrive. An operator can refuse or stall, and a contract or a treaty has to say what follows.
    • That nobody can forge one. Soundness rests on cryptographic assumptions, some of them conjectures, on code without bugs and, in some systems, on a trusted third party. SASH's publisher (new tab) writes that “bugs in deployed proof systems have let provers prove false statements before”.

    Today's systems

    Proving a reply costs a large multiple of serving it. Checking is not always faster than rerunning the model on a GPU. The ladder prints that cost and the verify time of every system we ran, and the reason each other system was not run.

    FlashProve's README (new tab) calls it “A research artifact: not maintained, not accepting contributions.” SASH's publisher (new tab) calls it an “open-source ZKP prover measurement prototype”. Nobody else has rerun any of our runs (how to check us).

    The ladder's models all have open weights: nothing here measures a closed model.

    Passed

    Passed means all six of our checks passed. No check attempts a forged proof or tests whether the weights stay hidden. The glyph grades the publisher's statements on both.

    The Model check changes one weight, Prompt one prompt token and Reply one reply token, and no change may end in an accepted proof. A trial counts only if the same run, unchanged, is accepted. A passed check shows that those changes were refused, and no more. Each counted proof must also be verified with no GPU visible and no model file on the machine (Verify without weights).
